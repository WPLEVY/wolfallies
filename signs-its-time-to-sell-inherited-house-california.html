Signs It's Time to Sell an Inherited House in California

By William B. Plevy, California Attorney & Real Estate Broker · DRE #01956776 Updated June 2026
The Short Answer

The right time to sell an inherited California home is usually soon after inheriting, while the stepped-up basis minimizes capital gains tax, before carrying costs accumulate significantly, and before family dynamics around the property create conflict. Practical signals that favor selling include: difficulty agreeing among co-heirs, inability to manage the property remotely, and inability to afford ongoing carrying costs.

The strongest financial signal to sell is the stepped-up basis window. Your cost basis was reset to fair market value on the date of death. Every month you hold the property, California real estate appreciation adds to your eventual capital gains exposure. Selling relatively soon after inheriting, while the sale price is close to the stepped-up basis, is typically the most tax-efficient strategy.

Carrying costs accumulate quickly on a vacant inherited property. Property taxes, homeowner's insurance, utilities, landscaping, and ongoing maintenance can easily exceed $2,000-$5,000 per month on a higher-value California home. Every month of delay is money leaving the estate. If the property is generating no income and costs are mounting, the economic case for selling sooner rather than later is strong.

Family conflict is a strong practical signal. When multiple heirs have different opinions about what to do with the property, one wants to keep it, another wants to rent it, a third wants to sell immediately, the longer the decision is delayed, the worse the conflict tends to get. A clean sale with proceeds divided typically resolves the conflict in a way that keeping or renting cannot.

Remote management is genuinely difficult. If you live in Northern California and the inherited property is in Los Angeles, managing it as a rental from a distance involves tenant selection, maintenance coordination, and legal compliance with California's complex landlord-tenant laws. The hassle and risk of remote landlording often exceeds the income benefit.

Under California's Proposition 19 (effective February 2021), the property tax reassessment exclusion for inherited property only applies if the inheriting child makes it their primary residence within one year. If you don't intend to live there, you will face full reassessment to current market value, dramatically increasing your property tax bill. This removes a previously significant financial incentive to keep inherited California property.
